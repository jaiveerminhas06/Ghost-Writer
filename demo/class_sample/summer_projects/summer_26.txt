While summer is a time for relaxation and vacation from the regular school routine, requiring some type of learning activity over the summer break can help students maintain and build upon the knowledge and skills they developed over the school year. There are reasonable arguments on both sides of this issue regarding whether summer projects should be teacher-designed or student-designed. 

Teacher-designed projects have the benefit of being tailored to directly support what students have been learning in their classes that school year. by assigning reading assignments, problem sets, oral history interviews or other targeted educational tasks, teachers can help reinforce important concepts and prepare students for the next grade level. This approach also helps ensure students are exposed to a diverse variety of topics and doesn't rely solely on their own interests or initiative. However, mandating very specific assignments from teachers runs the risk of feeling like an unwanted extension of the normal school workload during vacation time. It also doesn't allow students as much freedom to explore subjects they find genuinely interesting or engaging on a personal level.

Alternatively, allowing students to design their own summer learning projects within general guidelines empowers them to study what captures their own curiosity and creativity. By choosing their own topics, readings and activities, students are more invested in the work. They may be inspired to learn beyond what is required for a grade when pursuing self-directed interests. However, completely open-ended options run the risk of students selecting activities that provide little educational value or are too easy to complete. Without supervision, students also could fail to build upon skills and content from the previous school year if they choose unrelated topics. 

An ideal compromise is a hybrid model that balances teacher input with student choice. For example, teachers could provide a list of pre-approved project options directly tied to course curriculum, but also allow an open category for student-proposed ideas that meet basic learning objectives. A combination of mandatory and flexible assignments prevents learning loss over breaks while still cultivating independence and enthusiasm for learning outside the classroom experience. Overall communication between educators and students is key to developing summer work that achieves educational enrichment in a way that is engaging rather than like more school.
